# KD20 — Core Rules
**Version: v0.40 — 2026-10-08**

*Official KD20 ruleset. This is canon. Anything prefixed `MYD20_Resource` is inspiration only, not a rule.*

---

## Design Pillars

These are the north star. When a rules decision is unclear, the answer is whichever option best serves these pillars.

### 1. Zero to Hero, Earned
KD20 is built for the long character journey — from ordinary beginnings to legendary capability, over a campaign that takes real time to walk. Power is earned through play, not handed out. A character's growth should feel like a story the table lived through, not a spreadsheet filling in. Early fragility is a feature: it makes later mastery mean something.

A corollary: **exceptional nature is earned in play, not chosen at creation.** KD20 characters start as ordinary people struggling toward a destiny — not as half-giants, monstrous hybrids, or elementally-tainted prodigies who begin the story already exceptional. Origins like that front-load the very arc the game exists to make you walk. The oversized and the monstrous belong on the **threat track** (see Actors and Construction Tracks), where they are the world the heroes rise to meet — not the starting point players skip ahead to.

### 2. Genre-Portable Core
The engine is genre-agnostic. Fantasy is the first setting KD20 ships with, but the core resolution, progression, and character-building systems must adapt cleanly to Sci-Fi, Sci-Fantasy, and Steampunk without being rebuilt. Genre lives in the *content layer* — the equipment, the callings, the flavour — while the *engine layer* stays constant. A rule that only makes sense in fantasy belongs in the fantasy content module, not the core.

### 3. Drama From the Dice
The dice are the primary engine of tension and surprise. Rolls should produce a spectrum of outcomes, not a binary pass/fail, so that every throw can bend the story. Big swings — triumph and disaster — are part of the fun and should stay possible at every level.

### 4. The Table Decides Its Own Tone
KD20 supports many tones — gritty survival, heroic adventure, larger-than-life cinematic play — through deliberate up-front dials rather than one baked-in assumption. The default is a middle path, but the table tunes the game to the campaign it wants.

*(Pillars are living — we revise them if play teaches us better ones.)*

## How to Read These Rules

KD20 defines **when things happen** and **how they work** so that everyone at the table shares a common understanding. But definitions are reference points, not straitjackets.

**The GM adjudicates.** Where a rule calls for a judgment — is this a setting change? is that setback proportionate? does this roll even need to be made? — the GM makes the call, guided by the definitions in these rules and by what serves the game.

**The definitions serve the conversation.** Their purpose is shared ground: because the table can see the same definition the GM is working from, a disagreement has something concrete to be *about*. A player can point to the definition and say "by this, the situation didn't really change" — and now there's a real discussion, not a clash of vibes.

**The table is the backstop.** KD20 deliberately does not try to mechanically prevent every possible abuse. That path bloats the system and quietly insults the people playing it. The safeguard against a bad ruling is the table talking it through; the last resort, always available, is to stop playing and run the game you'd rather run. Trust, with a social check on the trust — not a rule for every contingency.

This stance applies everywhere below. Individual rules state their definitions and then trust the GM to apply them; they will not keep re-explaining this.

## The Core Engine

### The Basic Roll

**Roll 1d20, add your relevant bonus, and compare the total to a target number (the Difficulty, or DC) set by the GM.**

This single procedure resolves everything uncertain — attacks, skill use, saves, contests. If the outcome isn't in doubt, don't roll; the GM only calls for a roll when failure is possible and interesting. **KD20 has no trivial rolls: if something has no stakes, you narrate it, you don't roll it.**

### The Outcome Ladder

KD20 rolls don't just pass or fail. A result is read by **how far it clears or misses the target** — the margin, not the face of the die. This makes the *degree* of success matter, and it works identically against a fixed DC or against another actor's total.

| Result | Outcome | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Total beats the target by **20 or more** | **Supreme** | The best result — a triumph beyond what was attempted |
| Total beats the target by **10 or more** | **Decisive** | A clean, emphatic success with something extra |
| Total **meets or beats** the target (margin 0 to +9) | **Success** | You do the thing |
| Total **misses** the target (margin −1 to −9) | **Failure** | You don't — and a failure should cost or complicate |
| Total **misses by 10 or more** (margin −10 or worse) | **Failure + Hard Choice** | A decisive failure — it fails *and* opens the Hard Choice (below) |

**No die face is ever special — not at the top of the ladder, and not at the bottom.** There is no lucky-20 and no unlucky-1. A natural 20 is simply your highest roll and a natural 1 your lowest; neither decides an outcome on its own. *Everything* is read from the margin — a legendary result comes from a legendary *margin* (genuine capability against a genuine target), and a catastrophic one from missing by a wide *margin*. The die face is only ever an input to the total, never a verdict.

This makes the ladder **symmetric**: just as beating the target by 10 earns a Decisive and by 20 a Supreme, *missing* by 10 is a decisive failure that opens the **Hard Choice**. The same magnitude step (10) governs both exceptional success and exceptional failure.

**"The target"** is the DC in a roll against the world, or the opposing total in a contest. Everything about the ladder reads the same way against either.

### Zero to Hero, Written Into the Math

Because the ladder is pure margin, results scale with the character automatically. Early on — low bonuses against hard targets — clearing a DC by 10 is an achievement, clearing by 20 is rare and precious, and *missing* by 10 (a Hard Choice) is a real risk. As a character grows and their bonuses climb, the good margins come into reach more often and the bad ones recede, so Decisive and Supreme results bloom — and decisive failures fade — *as the character becomes heroic*. No separate rule is needed; Pillar 1 falls straight out of the arithmetic.

### The Hard Choice — Turning a Decisive Failure to Fortune

A **decisive failure** — a roll that misses the target by **10 or more** — fails like any other, but it also opens a bargain. This is the **Hard Choice**: the chance to walk away from a bad miss with something to show for it, at a price. It is the downside mirror of a Decisive success: the same 10-point margin that would have earned an emphatic win, in the other direction, earns a chance at fortune from the wreckage.

**Base rule — the offer.** On a failure by 10 or more, the GM may offer the player a **setback** — a complication that fits how the miss played out in the fiction (knocked down and must spend a Move to rise; a dropped or fouled tool; an opening that lets a nearby enemy strike or reposition; a lock that jams; a lie that lands badly). The player then chooses:

- **Accept** the setback and gain **1 Action Die**, or
- **Refuse** — the roll is simply a Failure, with no setback and no die.

Refusing is always safe. The failure already happened; the Hard Choice only ever adds an *optional* trade on top of it. The setback is the GM's to define and should suit the moment — the fiction of the bad miss drives it.

**Action Dice can pull a roll out of the Hard Choice band.** Because Action Dice are spent on the total and the ladder is pure margin, spending dice to raise a roll above the −10 line removes the decisive failure (and its Hard Choice) exactly as spending to reach a higher tier works anywhere else — all paths to a margin are equal. This gives the pool a second use beyond turning failures into successes: when you can't save the roll, you can still spend to keep a disaster from becoming a *catastrophe*. (A natural 1 is no longer special — it is simply the lowest roll, and like any roll it only opens the Hard Choice if its final margin is −10 or worse.)

The Hard Choice applies to any roll, in or out of combat.

**Optional rule — GM Imposition.** If the table agrees to it at Session Zero (see dials), the GM gains an additional tool: at a moment of their choosing, instead of *offering* a Hard Choice, the GM may **impose** one — the setback happens whether the player likes it or not, and the player receives **2 Action Dice** in exchange. There is no refusal.

This does not replace the base rule. A GM with this permission still hands out consent-based Hard Choices normally; imposition is a card they can play when the moment calls for it — a fumble that *should* cost something, softened by a larger reward for the loss of choice. The decision to allow the GM to operate this way is an up-front, in-the-open Session Zero agreement, so no player is ever blindsided by the *possibility*. When and how often it's used within that permission is the GM's call.

Setback severity is left to the GM in both modes. KD20 does not cap it mechanically — a good GM keeps setbacks proportionate to the moment (see *How to Read These Rules*).

### Decisive and Supreme Rewards

A **Decisive** result (beat the target by 10+) or a **Supreme** result (beat by 20+) does more than succeed — it pays out. What it pays depends on whether the roll was an attack or a skill use.

**In combat (attack rolls):** a Decisive or Supreme hit pays out in two possible currencies — a **Precision Die** (extra damage) and a **Follow-Up** (a combat effect or maneuver). The tier decides how many you get:

| Tier | Reward |
|---|---|
| Decisive | **One** of: a Precision Die on the damage roll, *or* a Follow-Up |
| Supreme | **Both**: a Precision Die on the damage roll *and* a Follow-Up |

The **Precision Die** is a bonus damage die reflecting a blow that found something vital — rolled and added to normal damage. It is always **a single die** (there is no doubled-damage tier); its size is a property of the weapon (default: equal to the weapon's damage die; some weapons differ — see *The Precision Die*). Supreme's upgrade over Decisive is not *more damage* — it is getting the Follow-Up *as well as* the die, instead of choosing between them.

**The Follow-Up** is a combat effect or maneuver that rides on top of the strike, and it comes packed with one rule: **it must flow causally from the action that spawned it.** A punishing maul-blow can send a foe sprawling or reeling; a punch can stun or stagger but cannot pin someone to a wall; a rapier thrust can open a riposte but cannot shove a heavy foe across the room. The GM adjudicates the fit — the effect has to be a plausible *consequence* of that specific attack.

- If the Follow-Up is a **flat effect** (a penalty imposed on the target), the **Modifier Ladder** gives its default size — the GM prices it as a Solid (−3) or Major (−5) hindrance to fit the moment.
- If the Follow-Up is a **maneuver** (knockdown, shove, disarm, throw), it is a **free maneuver attempt riding on the same action** — normally a maneuver would cost your action, but the great hit earns you the attempt on top of the strike. It is **rolled to resolve** like any maneuver (a contest), so it can still fail.

Combat rewards stay transient — a Precision Die or a Follow-Up, never an Action Die; they do not feed the pool.

**In skill use (non-attack rolls):** a Decisive or Supreme result **improves the outcome itself** — it does not award Action Dice.

| Tier | Result |
|---|---|
| Decisive | The success comes with something extra — the lock opens *and* you spot the compartment behind it; the negotiation doesn't just succeed, it warms the room |
| Supreme | A much greater success, or an additional benefit on top — the negotiation flips the NPC fully to your side; the climb is so clean you're in position for a free action |

How much better a Decisive or Supreme skill result is falls to the GM, guided by the fiction (see *How to Read These Rules*). The two rungs mirror combat's structure — Decisive is "better," Supreme is "much better / additional" — but on the skill side the reward is always *a better outcome now*, never a die for the pool.

**The skill side has no die/effect choice.** There is nothing to choose or trade: the improved outcome *is* the reward. The Precision-Die-or-Follow-Up choice exists only in combat (see the combat table above); a skill Decisive/Supreme simply makes the result better.

### Bonuses

The bonus added to a d20 roll is a flat number, built from the character's **stat bonus** plus any **Specialization Die** that fires (see *Capabilities and the Anatomy of a Roll*). KD20 uses **flat modifiers** plus at most one conditional die, not stacked dice, as the baseline — this keeps totals fast to compute and the outcome spectrum easy to read at a glance. The Action Die is the one deliberate exception: a spendable, dramatic layer on top.

### Contests

When two actors oppose each other directly, both roll d20 + relevant bonus. **Higher total wins; ties favour the defender** (the one resisting or reacting).

The margin between the two totals reads on the same ladder as everything else, with **the opponent's total serving as the target**: win by 10+ and it's a **Decisive** win, win by 20+ and it's **Supreme**. An attack contest pays out in Precision Dice (or a waived effect); a skill contest pays out in a better outcome.

Because the winner is decided by magnitude *first*, there is never a paradox where a lower roll "out-tiers" a higher one: whoever rolled higher wins, and then that winning margin determines whether the win was merely a Success, Decisive, or Supreme. Luck is never a gate that lets a smaller number beat a bigger one — the bigger number always wins, and the margin just decorates it.

## The Modifier Ladder

The Success / Decisive / Supreme scale is KD20's **universal unit of magnitude**. It does not only read *outcomes* — it also **prices modifiers**. Any advantage or hindrance on a roll is graded on the same three rungs, in either direction:

| Magnitude | Advantage | Hindrance |
|---|---|---|
| Minor (Success-tier) | **+1** | **−1** |
| Solid (Decisive-tier) | **+3** | **−3** |
| Major (Supreme-tier) | **+5** | **−5** |

This gives the GM one number-language for every "this helps / this hurts" ruling. Rather than inventing modifiers ad hoc, the GM asks a single question — *how much does this matter: a little, a lot, or enormously?* — and the answer is always ±1 / ±3 / ±5.

### Two Sources of a Modifier

A modifier reaches a roll one of two ways:

- **Assessed** — the GM prices a circumstance directly at a tier, no roll required. *"The natural handholds aid you decisively — +3 to the climb."* *"The rain-slick ledge and the dark work against you — −3."* Like all GM rulings, assessed modifiers are governed by the trust-the-GM stance (the GM also sets the DC, so generous circumstances and a stiff target net out).
- **Earned** — an actor rolls a relevant skill to *aid* or *impede*, and their **outcome tier sets the modifier they lend**: a Success grants ±1, a Decisive ±3, a Supreme ±5. *"Your ally's Might lift beat the target by 20 — a Supreme assist, +5 to your own climb."* An earned attempt that **fails** grants ±0, and the actor has spent their action trying.

To aid or impede, the actor's contribution must be *plausible* in the fiction — a relevant skill, a sensible approach, an actual opening. The GM gates what can meaningfully help or hinder.

### The Cap and Netting

- **Each direction caps at ±5 on a single check**, counting all sources together (assessed and earned alike). Three villagers and a set of handholds cannot pile past +5; one expert rolling Supreme already reaches the ceiling. Help and hindrance matter without snowballing.
- **Advantage and hindrance then net against each other.** Cap each side at ±5 first, then sum: +5 aid and −3 hindrance → net **+2**; +3 and −3 → net **+0**.

### Worked Example — Aiding Recovery

Healing shows the ladder as a general tool, not a special case. A character's own natural healing is a **Recovery** roll (their body mending itself — a Constitution matter). Another character may apply the **Medicine** skill to *aid* that roll: they roll Medicine, and their tier lends the standard modifier — Success +1, Decisive +3, Supreme +5 — to the patient's next Recovery roll, capped with any other aid at +5. Nothing here is unique to medicine; it is the Modifier Ladder pointed at the recovery system. *(The recovery system itself is deferred; this shows how aid attaches to it once it exists.)*

## States and the Two Levers

Combat and magic constantly impose effects — knocked down, blinded, held, hasted, frightened, sharpened. KD20 calls any such effect a **State**, and handles *all* of them through **one model with two levers.** This replaces the bulky per-condition severity tables of older systems: there is no matrix to look up, because a State is just a short definition that pulls one or both of two levers.

### The Two Levers

Every State affects the rules in one of two ways — or both:

- **The Modifier lever** — the State changes a *number* on a roll, priced on the **Modifier Ladder** (−1/−3/−5 or +1/+3/+5). The character can still do the thing; they are just better or worse at it. *Impaired vision is −3 to sight-based rolls. Eagle-eyed is +5 to visual Perception. Shaken is −3 on rolls made toward the source of fear.*
- **The Permission lever** — the State changes *what the character is allowed to do*: it prohibits an action, alters how an action works, requires an action, or opens a new one. No roll is modified; a permission is switched. *Blind: no sight-based rolls at all. Held: cannot move. Stunned: lose your Action this round. Slow: only a Move or an Action, not both.*

A single State may pull **both** levers. **Prone** is the classic case: it changes permissions (you're on the ground; you must spend movement to stand) *and* bends dice (melee attackers over you gain a Modifier-Ladder edge). Its one-line definition simply names both.

### The GM's Question

To adjudicate any effect — named below, imported from a spell, or invented on the spot — the GM asks one thing:

> **Does this change a *number* on a roll, or change what the character is *allowed to do*?**
> **Number → Modifier lever.** Apply a Ladder value (−1/−3/−5).
> **Permission → Permission lever.** State plainly what they now can't do, must do, or may now do — and don't roll the impossible.

That single question resolves everything. A GM never needs a severity chart; they need only decide which lever an effect pulls, and apply it in that lever's native way.

### Why There Is No Severity Scale

Older systems graded each condition across many rungs ("Blinded, moderate" vs. "Blinded, severe"), which forced a lookup for every application. KD20 doesn't, because **gradability lives only on the Modifier lever.** "How much" is *always* the Ladder's three steps (−1/−3/−5) and nowhere else. Permission-States are **binary** — you can or you can't; there is no "somewhat Blind." If an effect feels partial, that is the *tell* that it belongs on the Modifier lever, not that it needs a milder rung: *impaired vision* (Modifier, −3) and *Blind* (Permission, can't see) are **two different States pulling two different levers**, not one condition at two volumes. Keeping the levers separate is what makes the severity matrix unnecessary.

### Named States (the standard set)

Each is one line: its lever(s), its effect, and **how it ends**. This is the core roster; a setting or spell may add more, and a GM may invent their own (see below).

- **Prone** *(Permission + Modifier)* — you are on the ground; melee attackers over you gain **+3**; you must spend movement to stand. *Ends:* stand up (costs part of your Move).
- **Blind** *(Permission)* — you cannot see; no sight-based rolls, and you cannot make ranged attacks. *Ends:* per the effect that caused it (a turn, a heal, clearing the smoke).
- **Deafened** *(Permission)* — you cannot hear; no hearing-based rolls, and effects needing speech may fail. *Ends:* per its cause.
- **Held / Restrained** *(Permission)* — you cannot move from your space; you may still act. *Ends:* break free (a contest as your Action) or the effect lapses.
- **Disarmed** *(Permission)* — your weapon is gone from hand; you may not attack with it until recovered. *Ends:* spend an Action (and possibly a Move) to retrieve or ready another.
- **Slow** *(Permission)* — you get **either** a Move **or** an Action this round, not both. *Ends:* per its cause (usually end of your next turn, or a resistance roll).
- **Stunned** *(Permission)* — you lose your Action this round and cannot take reactions. *Ends:* end of the round.
- **Dying** *(Permission — the death track)* — the state of a character who Went Down at 0 HP: unconscious, helpless, on the Golden-Hour clock. *Ends:* stabilised, healed, or rescued before the clock runs out (see *Hit Points, Recovery, and Defeat*). *(The Recovery system that governs longer-term healing and the Wound/injury catalogue is still deferred.)*

*(Frightened, Charmed, Confused, Exhausted, Poisoned and their kin from older material are mostly **Modifier-lever** effects in KD20 — a −3 toward the source, a −3 on the relevant rolls — unless they cross a true permission threshold ("cannot approach," "cannot attack the caster"), at which point they become a Permission-State with that specific prohibition. Author them by asking the GM's Question, not by copying a severity table.)*

### GM-Authored States

A GM may invent a State for anything the fiction demands. To keep it fast and unambiguous, a home-made State must state the same three things every standard State does: **which lever(s) it pulls, its exact effect, and how it ends.** A State without a named end-condition is a trap — always define how a character gets out of it. If it can be described as "worse at X," it is a Modifier-lever effect (use the Ladder); if it "can't / must / may now," it is a Permission-State (name the prohibition or permission).

### Authoring Effects (spells, feats, weapon properties)

This model is the shared vocabulary for *every* effect in KD20, not just combat conditions. When a spell, feat, or weapon property imposes something, it is written in exactly these terms — a **Modifier value**, a **named State**, **damage**, or a combination:

- *Hold Person* imposes **Held** (Permission).
- *Slow* imposes **Slow** (Permission — Move or Action, not both).
- *Eagle Eyes* grants **+5 to visual Perception** (Modifier).
- *Fireball* deals **damage**, and on a Decisive/Supreme may add **Prone** (Follow-Up, Permission).

Because every effect resolves to the Modifier lever, the Permission lever, or damage, no spell or feat needs bespoke machinery — and the outcome ladders of imported material (crit/fumble tables and the like) re-express cleanly as "on a Decisive, add this State / this Ladder value." *(The spell system itself is deferred; this is the vocabulary it will be built in.)*

### Effect Escalation — How Non-Damage Effects Scale With Success

When an actor rolls against a defense (see *Defenses*), the result reads on the outcome ladder — and the **effect scales with the tier**, exactly as damage does. Damage is the familiar case: Success deals base damage, **Decisive** adds a Precision Die (or a Follow-Up), **Supreme** adds both. Non-damage effects (fear, poison, a shove, a social jab) scale the same way, through a **default escalation ladder** so most effects need only name their *base* and their *escalation axis* — the ladder handles the rest, just as the Modifier Ladder prices all modifiers and the margin ladder handles all Precision/Follow-Up.

**The default ladder:**

| Tier | Non-damage effect | (Damage parallel) |
|---|---|---|
| **Success** | the effect's **base** (its stated State / Modifier value) | base damage |
| **Decisive** | escalate **one step** along the effect's axis | + a Precision Die |
| **Supreme** | escalate **two steps**, *or* one step **plus a rider** (a second State / Follow-Up) | + Precision Die **and** a Follow-Up |

**The escalation axes (the menu).** An effect names which axis (or axes) it escalates along; one step up that axis per tier:

- **Magnitude** — up the Modifier Ladder: Minor −1 → Solid −3 → Major −5. *(A Success Rattles for −1; Decisive −3; Supreme −5.)*
- **Severity** — cross levers, Modifier → Permission: a Success imposes a Modifier penalty; a Decisive/Supreme crosses the threshold into a Permission-State. *(Success: −3 "shaken" toward the source; Decisive: Frightened — cannot approach.)*
- **Duration** — up a time band: a round → the scene → extended. *(Success: Slowed this round; Decisive: Slowed the whole fight.)*
- **Scope** — one target → more: a Success hits the one; Decisive/Supreme catches additional targets (the natural axis for area/social-crowd effects).

An effect picks the axis that fits its fiction; the GM may substitute an equal-weight step where the fiction demands. **Supreme's "rider"** is the non-damage parallel of a Follow-Up: a second State, a longer duration *and* a penalty, or a spread to another target — the "everything" result.

**This keeps authoring light:** a spell or feat states "imposes *Rattled* (Magnitude axis)" and the ladder does Success −1 / Decisive −3 / Supreme −5 automatically — no per-spell outcome table. The escalation logic is one rule, shared by damage and effects alike, read off the margin like everything else in KD20.

## Action Dice

Action Dice are KD20's negotiation layer — a finite, spendable pool that lets a player push a roll harder when it matters. They are the deliberate exception to the flat-modifier baseline: the place where players spend a precious resource to reach for something more.

### The Pool

Every character has a pool of Action Dice. **Pool size (the refresh rate) is a Session Zero dial** — the table sets it to match the tone it wants:

| Dial | Refresh rate | Feel |
|---|---|---|
| **Gritty** | **3** | scarce; every die is precious |
| **Heroic** *(default)* | **5** | the standard KD20 pool |
| **Cinematic** | **8** | generous, larger-than-life |

*(Numbers provisional; Heroic 5 is simulation-anchored — see the Playtest Watch-List.)*

**Refresh — default (floor-raising).** At the start of each session the pool is set to the **greater of** the dice carried over or the refresh rate: a depleted pool tops back up to the rate, and a pool that ran *above* the rate (from dice earned in play) keeps the surplus. Earned dice are never confiscated by a session boundary.

This is the default for two reasons beyond simple fairness. First, **sessions often end mid-course** — mid-scene, mid-fight, on a cliffhanger — and a hard wipe would routinely erase dice players were partway through using. Second, and more important, it **preserves end-of-session rewards**: a GM can hand out an Action Die for a great moment in the last ten minutes (a Cool! die, a milestone, a Hard Choice payoff) and have it actually *matter*, because it survives to next session. A hard reset makes late-session rewards worthless, quietly breaking the earning economy at exactly the climactic beats where rewarding bold play matters most. Floor-raising keeps that discretion in the GM's hands, consistent with KD20's trust-the-GM philosophy. (Hoarding is not a concern: the simulation shows piling dice up is self-defeating anyway — overflow past a +20 margin is wasted — so the spend-freely incentive holds without a forced wipe.)

**Refresh — optional variant (hard reset).** A table that specifically wants a strict spend-or-lose feel may instead reset the pool to the refresh rate each session, discarding any surplus. Chosen at Session Zero. (Trade-off: end-of-session dice awards lose their value, so time rewards earlier in the session.)

### Spending Action Dice

You may spend one or more Action Dice on a roll, **before or after seeing the result** — declare, roll the die/dice, and add each result to your total. **There is no cap on how many you spend on a single roll** beyond what's in your pool. When you spend several at once, you **roll that many d6 and sum them all** into the roll (spend 3 → roll 3d6, add the total). Each die is an independent d6; there is no chaining, stepping-up, or re-rolling — just more dice, summed.

Action Dice can be spent to:

- **Boost a roll** — add the Action Die result to a d20 roll you're making or just made.
- **Take a Reaction** — act outside your turn (see Action Economy, once written). One die buys one reactive moment.

*(Further spends — pushing magic, fuelling special abilities — will be defined as those systems are written. The spending framework here is the constant they'll build on.)*

### The Action Die is a d6

Every Action Die is a **d6**. Fixed — always. Nothing about a character's build, level, or gear changes the size of the die. Action Dice do not grow, boost, or step up a chain; a character gets *stronger* by having a higher flat bonus and more ways to earn and spend dice, not by rolling bigger ones.

**There is no cap on stacking d6s on a roll.** A player flush with dice can pour several onto a single critical moment. The regulator on a fat pool is not a rule — it is *challenge*: raise the difficulty and the dice get spent as fast as they come in. A hoarding party is an invitation for the GM to bring something that makes them glad they saved up.

### Earning Action Dice in Play

The pool can be topped up mid-session (above max, until refresh). In KD20, **dice come from fiction and character, never from raw mechanical success** — you don't earn a die by rolling well, you earn it by playing your character, thrilling the table, hitting story beats, or triggering a build you invested in. The general principle: **earning an Action Die requires genuine cost, risk, commitment, or a moment worth rewarding.** The GM is the arbiter.

Four earning sources:

- **Leaning into a negative trait.** When the GM leans on one of a character's negative traits to complicate a scene and the player *accepts* the consequence, the player earns **1 Action Die**. This is the sibling of the Hard Choice — accept an in-fiction setback for a die — but sourced from *who the character is* rather than a decisive failure. *(Depends on the traits system, deferred; the earning hook is fixed here.)*
- **Table acclaim — the "Cool!" die.** When a player does something that makes the table or the GM light up, award **1 Action Die**. No checklist by design — the table knows it when they see it. For genuine moments, not participation.
- **Milestones.** GMs are encouraged to seed milestones in their scenarios; when the party reaches one, **every party member earns 1 or more Action Dice** (suggested 1–3, GM's call). This is the authored-pacing lever — a deliberate way to refill the party's tanks before a set-piece.
- **Feats that grant dice.** Certain feats (e.g. a "Luck" feat) grant an Action Die to the pool when their narrowly-defined trigger fires. *(Depends on the feat system, deferred; each such feat must define a tight trigger. Note: this is distinct from a Specialization Die — see Specialization, deferred — which is a conditional bonus die on the roll, not a pool die.)*

*(The trait-die and feat-die hooks await their owning systems. Table acclaim and milestones are usable as written.)*

## Statistics and Bonuses

Every character is described by six **Statistics**. A Statistic is the raw score; the **stat bonus** is the flat number *derived* from it, and the stat bonus is what actually goes onto a d20 roll.

### The Six Statistics

| Statistic | Bonus (short form) |
|---|---|
| Strength | str |
| Dexterity | dex |
| Constitution | con |
| Intelligence | int |
| Wisdom | wis |
| Charisma | cha |

A Statistic is written out and capitalized (**Strength**). Its derived bonus is written as the **lowercase three-letter short form** (**str**). This distinction is exact and load-bearing: **when a rule asks for "str," it is asking for the bonus, never the raw Statistic.** "Add str to the roll" means add the derived bonus.

**The character of each Statistic** — *what each one governs.* This is the **engine of the whole game's "which bonus do I roll?" question**, far more than any list of named activities: a GM who knows what each stat *is* can assign any action to a stat on the spot, and never needs a skill list to look it up (see *Capability Roll-Types*, below). Each stat also owns one Save (see *Defenses*).

- **Strength (str)** — raw physical force and power. **Governs:** melee force, lifting, breaking, climbing, grappling, hauling, any feat of muscle. Powers most melee damage, and the **Strength Save** against being shoved, grappled, tripped, or moved against your will.
- **Dexterity (dex)** — agility, speed, and precision. **Governs:** balance, tumbling, sleight of hand, stealthy movement, aim, finesse, reflexive dodging, anything needing a steady or quick hand. Feeds AC and most ranged/finesse attacks, and the **Dexterity Save** to dodge clear of blasts, areas, and traps.
- **Constitution (con)** — toughness, stamina, and vitality. **Governs:** endurance, holding your breath, resisting fatigue and the elements, going without food or sleep, simply outlasting. Anchors HP, and the **Constitution Save** against poison, disease, exhaustion, and raw bodily assault.
- **Intelligence (int)** — the *learned* mind: knowledge, reason, and study. **Governs:** recall and lore, languages, deduction and logic, crafting and engineering, arcane theory — anything *studied, reasoned out, or known*. Powers learned arcane capability, and the **Intelligence Save** guarding your *thoughts* (telepathy, memory-tampering, mind-reading, logic-scrambling confusion).
- **Wisdom (wis)** — the *sensing* mind: perception, judgment, and intuition. **Governs:** noticing and searching, reading people and intent, tracking, survival and animal handling, gut judgment — anything *perceived or felt in the moment* rather than studied. Feeds initiative and awareness, and the **Wisdom Save** guarding your *clarity* (seeing through illusion and deception, sensing that something is wrong, spotting the trap).
- **Charisma (cha)** — force of personality, presence, and will. **Governs:** persuasion, intimidation, deception, performance, leadership, commanding a room — any bending of others by sheer self. Powers social capability, and the **Charisma Save** guarding your *self* (defiance of domination, compulsion, possession, fear that would break you, and the crowd that would turn you).

*(The classic **Int vs. Wis** line, stated once so it never needs re-arguing: **Intelligence is what you learned; Wisdom is what you sense.** Recalling a monster's weakness from study is Int; *noticing* the same weakness in the moment is Wis. Knowing the law is Int; reading whether the judge believes you is Wis.)*

### Deriving the Bonus

The stat bonus is the standard d20 derivation:

**bonus = (Statistic − 10) ÷ 2, rounded down.**

So Strength 14 → str +2; Strength 10 → str +0; Strength 7 → str −2. A Statistic below 10 yields a penalty.

| Statistic | Bonus |
|---|---|
| 1 | −5 |
| 2–3 | −4 |
| 4–5 | −3 |
| 6–7 | −2 |
| 8–9 | −1 |
| 10–11 | +0 |
| 12–13 | +1 |
| 14–15 | +2 |
| 16–17 | +3 |
| 18–19 | +4 |
| 20 | +5 |

### Range at Creation

Statistics are **rolled** at character creation. Two independent choices, both made at Session Zero, set how:

**1. The dice — how high, on average.**
- **3d6** per Statistic — grittier, lower average, a more fragile and hard-scrabble start.
- **4d6 drop the lowest** — heroic, higher average, more capable heroes from the outset.

**2. The method — how the rolls are distributed.**
- **Individual** — each player rolls their own array of six and keeps it (assigning the numbers to Statistics as they like).
- **Shared Pool** — everyone rolls an array of six (using the chosen dice), and **every array is laid on the table for the whole party.** Any player may adopt **any** array as their own — *duplicates are fine*, even the whole party taking the same one — and then assigns those six numbers to their own Statistics individually. Because your own rolled array is always on the table, "keep mine" is simply one of the choices; no one is ever forced off their own dice.

The Shared Pool exists to solve a KD20-specific pain: Statistics are hard to raise (see *Raising a Statistic* — catalyst-gated, not bought), so a bad starting roll would sting for a long time. Pooling lets the unlucky adopt the best array *anyone* rolled, which keeps the fun of rolling while removing its punishment, and makes creation a shared table moment. It has **no cap** — the best available array naturally improves with party size, which suits a heroic game; a table wanting stat scarcity and the drama of a lived-with bad roll simply uses the Individual method (and/or 3d6). The two knobs combine freely: *Shared Pool + 3d6* is fair-but-gritty; *Shared Pool + 4d6* is fair-and-heroic.

Whatever the method, creation adjustments are small (at most a **+2** to any one Statistic from creation sources). Between the dice and that small bump, **20 is the effective ceiling at creation** — not because a rule forbids higher, but because the generation method cannot reach it. Higher Statistics are possible later, through the advancement described below.

### Raising a Statistic

Statistics do not rise on an open XP purchase the way feats do. Raising a Statistic is a **catalyst-plus-consolidation** process with two parts:

- **The catalyst (GM-gated).** A Statistic increase must first be *earned in the fiction* — unlocked by the GM through a narrative trigger: a quest undertaken for it, a training arc under a master, a hard-won crucible survived, a boon received. **The pace of Statistic growth is the GM's to control** — the door only opens when the story opens it. A player cannot simply buy raw talent.
- **The consolidation (a low XP cost).** Once the catalyst opens the door, the character pays a **low** XP cost to complete the increase — representing the reps, practice, and internalization that turn opportunity into permanent change. This cost is deliberately small (roughly half a cluster feat, ~10 XP), because it is not the gate — the catalyst is. It is the nod to the work.

**Time is abstracted through XP.** Because XP is earned over real play, the low cost already encodes elapsed time — you cannot consolidate until you have adventured enough to afford it. A GM may additionally narrate a training interval ("you spend a month in the mountains") as flavor, but KD20 tracks no per-Statistic training durations — that granularity is deliberately avoided.

There is no hard ceiling; **20 remains the effective creation cap** (the dice can't exceed it), and growth past it is possible but rare, gated by ever-harder catalysts as the GM sees fit. *(Provisional consolidation cost ~10 XP — pegged low against the cluster feat, the XP economy's atomic unit.)*

### Where Bonuses Come From, and How Characters Grow

The bonus on any given d20 roll is a flat total built from the relevant **stat bonus** plus a **Specialization Die** when a feat's trigger fires. This is the flat-modifier baseline the whole outcome ladder reads against; the Action Die is the one spendable exception layered on top.

Character growth runs through **four distinct lanes**, and keeping them separate is what protects the game from power-creep. Each kind of "getting better" has exactly one home:

- **Height** — a higher permanent ceiling. This comes *only* from **raising a Statistic**, which is deliberately the hardest advancement to get (a GM-gated narrative catalyst plus a low XP consolidation; see *Raising a Statistic*). Flat, always-on power is rationed and earned through story.
- **Breadth** — more things you can *do*. This comes from **feats**: each adds a new trigger, option, permission, or consequence — never a bigger number at something you already do. Growth here is horizontal (more situations you're exceptional in), never a rising bonus. *(One sanctioned exception: a **Hardened Save** feat buys permanent flat height on a defensive Save — allowed because it is defensive, costed as height, and gated by the gateway Save profile. See Defenses.)*
- **Focus** — being better *right now, when it matters*. This comes from **spending Action Dice** — dynamic, dramatic, and earned-and-spent through play rather than fixed on the sheet.
- **Consistency** — a *less-bad worst case*, never a higher best case. This is the niche of the **Action-Die floor** carried by some gateway benefits: it raises the low end of a boost die on a signature act while leaving the ceiling untouched (anyone can still roll a 6). It models expertise-as-reliability — the expert doesn't have a higher peak, they simply whiff less often.

Two of these touch dice height only in *defanged* forms: the **Specialization Die** raises an average but is *random* (a d6, not a flat bonus) and *conditional* (only on its trigger); the **floor** shaves variance without ever raising the ceiling. The only source of clean, flat, permanent height is the Statistic line. Everything else is breadth, focus, or consistency — the same d6 atom under different conditions.

**XP is never refunded.** Spending XP buys *what you had and used* — not a cancelable reservation. If a purchase is later lost, broken, or abandoned (a devotion forsworn, a feat whose prerequisite you give up), you do **not** get the XP back: it paid for the capability you actually had while you had it, and ending that capability does not un-spend the time it represents. This mirrors the catalyst-plus-consolidation logic of Statistics (you can't un-live experience) and the Master Feat's permanence. It also means every "can I undo this?" question has the same answer — no refund — so it never needs re-litigating per feat.

## Capabilities and the Anatomy of a Roll

Everything that makes a character better at something is **a d6, added under a condition** — the atom of KD20. Capability is not a separate rank-based subsystem; it is denominated in that atom, the same as combat. "Good at fighting" and "good at lockpicking" are the same kind of thing.

### The Full Roll

Every d20 roll in KD20 assembles the same way:

**1d20 + stat bonus + Specialization Die (if a feat's trigger fires) — then adjusted by the Modifier Ladder (±1/±3/±5) — compared to a DC or an opposing roll.**

The **stat bonus** derives from the governing Statistic; the **Specialization Die** is a conditional d6 granted by a feat when its trigger is met; the **Modifier Ladder** prices circumstance and aid/hindrance. This is the complete anatomy of a KD20 roll — nothing else is added to it (the Action Die, the one spendable exception, layers on top when a player chooses to focus).

### The Three-Tier Capability Stack

Any task resolves through up to three layers:

1. **Base competence (anyone, no investment).** `1d20 + stat`. Any actor may attempt almost anything; stats are the universal floor, and untrained is not helpless. This is why no Statistic is ever "short on skills" — every stat is the floor for any task the fiction assigns it. *(Simulation: an untrained character at stat +2 succeeds ~65% at Easy, ~50% at Moderate, ~35% at Hard, ~15% at Formidable — playable at every tier, but training clearly matters.)*
2. **Trained identity (a feat's Specialization Die).** Training is a **feat**: a named, narrow trigger that grants **one d6** when its circumstance is met. Buy the feat, get the die on the trigger. *(Simulation: adding the on-trigger d6 at Moderate drops failure from ~50% to ~32% and opens ~17% Decisive — the die does everything a skill rank used to, with no rank ladder needed.)*
3. **Dynamic gradient (Action Dice).** How good you are *at this moment* is how much you commit — spend Action Dice to push the roll. This is the gradient that ranks used to provide, but player-controlled and dramatic. *(Simulation: a trained specialist at Moderate spending 0→4 dice climbs 68%→85%→95%→99%→~100% success — a smooth "how hard am I trying right now" dial.)*

### One Die Applies — and the Chosen Feat Sets the Fiction

Even when several of a character's feats *could* apply, **only one Specialization Die is ever added to a roll** — it would not be *specialization* if everything applied. Owning many feats means broader *coverage* (you are exceptional in more situations), never a bigger bonus in one.

When more than one feat could apply, the player chooses which die to add — and **that choice sets the fiction**: the invoked feat declares *how* the character succeeds. Getting past a guard with a Stealth feat is a scene of shadows and timing (he never knows you were there); with a Bribery feat it is coin and a corrupt witness who now knows your face; with a Seduction feat it is charm and a thread the GM may pull later. Same die, different scene — and the GM may let the invoked method shape the *consequences* (and the shape of failure). Overlap thus becomes a *choice of how you win*, not a redundant bonus. *(Design rule: author feats to be as unique as possible; minimize unavoidable overlap.)*

### Growth Is Breadth (with One Exception)

Feats are **bought once** — no tiers, no "Feat I / II / III." Character growth is *breadth* (more triggers, more situations you are exceptional in), never inflation (bigger numbers in one). The sole exception is the **Master Feat** (see *Gateways and Feats*): once, permanently, a character may narrow one feat they hold to a still-sharper signature trigger and grant *that* a second d6 — deeper specialization, not a bigger number, and the only place two Specialization Dice stack.

### Defenses — Static AC, Rolled Saves

KD20 splits defense into two kinds, by a line that matches how it *feels* to defend: **passive avoidance is static; active resistance is rolled.**

- **AC (Armour Class) is static** — being hard to *hit* is passive (you're armoured, you're nimble, the blow finds no opening). The attacker rolls against it. **AC = 10 + dex + armour + shield + misc.** Unchanged; this is the one defense the defender never rolls, because not-getting-hit isn't an act of will — it's a condition.
- **The six Saves are rolled** — resisting a *specific assault* on you (a grapple, a poison, a fear, a blast) is *active*: you brace, you dodge, you steel your mind. The dice belong in the defender's hands at that moment. Each Save is `1d20 + stat + gateway profile step + feats`, rolled against the attacker's total, read on the outcome ladder by margin.

**The six Saves — one per Statistic, named for the stat.** Each Save is simply *"[Statistic] Save"* — the stat name tells you which bonus it uses and, with the coverage below, what it defends:

| Save | Stat | Defends against |
|---|---|---|
| **Strength Save** | str | being shoved, grappled, tripped, overborne, moved against your will |
| **Dexterity Save** | dex | area effects, blasts, traps — anything you dodge clear of |
| **Constitution Save** | con | poison, disease, exhaustion, raw bodily assault |
| **Intelligence Save** | int | **the mind** — telepathy, mind-reading, memory tampering, logic-scrambling confusion |
| **Wisdom Save** | wis | **perception** — illusion, deception, sensing wrongness, spotting the trap |
| **Charisma Save** | cha | **the self / force of will** — domination, compulsion, possession, fear, intimidation, a crowd turning |

*The three mental Saves divide cleanly, no overlap: **Intelligence** guards the integrity of your* thoughts *(someone reaching into your mind); **Wisdom** guards the clarity of your* perception *(something trying to fool your senses or judgment); **Charisma** guards your* self *(something trying to override your will — to dominate, terrify, or break you). Charisma is force of personality on defense exactly as it is on offense: the Face bends others' wills, and the Charisma Save is the will that refuses to be bent.*

*(Bare stat names replace the earlier flavour names — retired synonyms, kept here once so older notes still parse: Strength Save = "Withstand," Dexterity = "Reflexes," Constitution = "Fortitude," Intelligence = "Composure," Wisdom = "Resolve"/"discernment," Charisma = "Poise"/"Resolve-of-will." The bare name is clearer at the table and makes the* Fitting Save *rule below legible — when which stat applies can shift, the plain stat name is the clearest possible label.)*

**Why the stat name, not a flavour name.** A new player reading "Charisma Save" knows instantly which bonus to add and, from the coverage line, what it covers — no vocabulary to memorize, no "is Endurance the con one or the str one?" The stat name also does real work for the *Fitting Save* rule (below): because the save that applies to a given threat can shift with the fiction, the clearest label is the one that names the stat you're actually rolling.

**AC is separate from the Saves.** AC uses dex + armour but is the "hard to hit" number, not any stat's Save. A gateway may make a calling hard to hit through a **cluster feat** that boosts AC — AC is built and invested in, not handed out in the free Save profile.

### The Fitting Save — When More Than One Save Could Apply

A threat names a **default Save** (the obvious one: a fireball → Dexterity, a fear-spell → Charisma, a grapple → Strength). But the world is not always obvious, and KD20's whole stance is that *the fiction leads*. So:

> **If a defender describes a defense that credibly routes the threat through a different Statistic, the GM may allow that Statistic's Save to apply instead of the default.**

This is the defensive mirror of the capability philosophy — anyone may attempt anything; your build just makes you *good* at it — and it is deliberately a piece of **GM craft, not a player entitlement.** The rules:

- **Fiction leads, the GM decides.** It is permission the GM *grants*, not a Save the player *picks*. If the described defense doesn't convince, the default Save stands. **No table debate — the GM's "no" is final and fast.** This stays a texture the GM reaches for, never a negotiation on every attack.
- **One Save, not best-of.** The Fitting Save *replaces* the default; you never roll two and take the higher. You commit to the approach and you roll it.
- **Earned by commitment — there is usually a cost or condition.** The alternative Save is bought with something concrete in the fiction: the greatshield has to be in hand and braced; the Monk's evasion needs room to move; hiding behind cover needs cover to hide behind. This is what keeps a gateway's mandatory −3 Save meaningful most of the time — you can route *around* your weak Save only when you've genuinely set up to.
- **The fiction's consequences stay — this is the key balancer.** Succeeding on a Fitting Save does **not** erase everything else that defense implies. The default Save is frequently *also* the "get clear" Save; the Fitting Save keeps you *alive*, not *clear*. Choosing a **Strength Save** to brace against a fireball rather than a **Dexterity Save** to dodge it means you *chose to stay in the area of effect* — you survive the blast, but the fire is still there: your cloak is smoking, the bridge you're standing on is burning, everything flammable around you caught. The GM narrates those consequences in full. You traded "away from the danger" for "through the danger, still standing."
- **It cuts both ways — threats use it too.** A monster may route a would-be Charisma (fear) effect through Constitution if the fiction fits ("it's not dread — it's a neurotoxin that *feels* like dread, resist it with your body"). The Fitting Save is a GM tool for making threats characterful, not a PC-only escape hatch.
- **Baseline is universal and free.** Any actor may invoke a Fitting Save with fiction + GM permission, exactly as maneuvers are universal. Feats may later grant *reliability* — making a particular substitution automatic and permission-free (e.g. a shield feat that always lets you brace a Strength Save vs. area effects) — but the baseline is open to everyone without investment.

**Worked example — the braced greatshield.** A Warrior (profile: Strength Save +3, Dexterity Save −3) faces a dragon's breath. Normally this is a **Dexterity Save** — his *worst* save, exactly the kind of area effect dex is for. Instead he plants his **large shield** (large shields only — no bucklers or small shields brace a breath weapon) and declares he's bracing behind it. The GM, convinced, lets him roll a **Strength Save** — trading his worst save for his best. But he *chose to stand in the fire*: he makes the save and survives the cone, yet he didn't move, the ground around him is scorched, and anything flammable on or near him is now burning. He bought survival with commitment and position, not a free upgrade. *(This is the canonical teaching example: the big shield, the stat-swap, the price paid in staying put.)*

### Contest or Save? The Active/Passive Line

Because Saves are rolled, "a resistance" and "a contest" become nearly the same act — and one line tells you which to use:

> **Passive target → roll against a static number. Active opposition → both roll (a contest).**

- A **Save** is the *active* case: the attacker is pushing an effect at you and you actively resist → effectively a contest (attacker's total vs. your Save roll). Fear spell → your **Charisma Save** vs. the caster's total. Poison → **Constitution Save**. Blast → **Dexterity Save**.
- A **grapple, shove, or trip** is active opposition → a **contest**: the attacker's maneuver roll vs. your **Strength Save**. (A "Strength Save" and "a contest against your Strength" are the same thing — that's the elegance.)
- **Stealth vs. an unaware guard** (passive target) → you roll Stealth vs. the guard's **static** Perception DC (`10 + wis`). The guard isn't actively looking, so they don't roll.
- **Stealth vs. a guard actively searching** → **contest**: your Stealth vs. their Perception roll. Both active.
- **A PC actively searching for a hidden foe** → **contest**: Perception vs. Stealth. Both active.

So: *is the target actively opposing this, right now?* Yes → contest (both roll). No → roll against their static number. AC is the one defense that's *always* static, because being-hard-to-hit is never an active moment.

**Action Dice work on defense exactly as on offense:** a Save is a roll, so you may spend Action Dice to boost it, after seeing the attacker's total — no special rule needed. (Parry and Ward are reactions that aid a defensive roll; they fit this naturally.) Against a static AC, the attacker is the one who may spend to push through.

### Gateway Save Profiles

A bare `10 + stat` Save would make every calling resist identically for a given stat — a bookish Scholar would shrug off fear exactly as well as a hardened Warrior. So each **gateway shapes its Save identity** by applying a fixed set of Modifier-Ladder steps across the six Saves (not AC — see below). A character's Save is:

**`Save = 1d20 + stat + gateway profile step (+ feats)`**, rolled against the attacker's total.

**The profile template is fixed — every gateway assigns exactly this shape across the six Saves, no more, no less:**
- **two Solid (+3)** — the calling's strong Saves
- **one Minor (+1)** — a secondary strength
- **two Unmodified (0)** — neutral
- **one Solid hindrance (−3)** — a real, exploitable soft spot

This is an *assignment*, not a budget to optimize — every gateway carries the identical total Save weight, only *distributed* differently to express the calling. There is no point-trading to exploit, and the mandatory −3 hindrance guarantees every calling has a genuine weakness a clever foe can target. (The *Fitting Save* rule lets a prepared character route *around* that −3 when the fiction earns it — at a cost — without erasing it.)

**AC is not part of the profile.** AC is the static "hard to hit" number (`10 + dex + armour`); a gateway that wants its calling to be hard to hit expresses that through a **cluster feat** that boosts AC, not through the free Save profile. AC is built and invested in; the Save profile is handed out.

*This is deliberate, because "how are you hard to hit?" is a characterful question with different answers per calling — and the two AC feats (authored in the Feats document) answer it two ways:*
- ***Armour Training** (Warrior, Guardian, future heavies): **+1 AC** from trained use of worn armour, **max +2** (two purchases), only while armour heavier than light is worn — the plate-knight fantasy, where the AC is in the steel and the feat is the mastery of it.*
- ***Unarmored Defense** (Monk, Rogue/Swashbuckler, Barbarian, future light fighters): while in **light or no armour**, AC becomes **`10 + dex + [the gateway's themed stat]`** — Monk **wis** (awareness as armour), Rogue/Swashbuckler **cha** (audacity), Barbarian **con** (toughness). The gateway names the stat, exactly as it names its casting stat. The slippery-dodger fantasy, where the AC is in *you*.*

*The two are **mutually exclusive by requirement** — one needs armour, the other its absence — so a character is one or the other, never both. Both are held in the static band: Armour Training by its +2 cap, Unarmored Defense by being self-limiting (two stats ≈ +5 each → ~20 at the extreme, the same band-top an agile-armoured veteran reaches). AC is **flat defensive height**, the AC sibling of Hardened Save, and the only other place a feat sells a flat bonus (see the Feats document's Design Principles).*

**The AC soft cap (~20).** Across *all* sources — base, dex, armour, shield, and the AC feats — a character's AC should land in the **~13–17 band**, reaching **~20 only at the extreme** (a veteran who is both agile and well-armoured, or a maxed unarmored fighter). **~20 is a design target the GM upholds, not a hard rule**: it is the ceiling the static-band promise depends on, and the AC feats are costed and capped to respect it. If a build somehow threatens to climb past ~20, that is the signal that something is stacking that shouldn't — not an invitation to raise DCs to chase it. (This mirrors the trust-the-GM stance everywhere else: the band is protected by design and judgment, not a hard wall.)

**Feats raise a Save one ladder step from its gateway baseline** (Unmodified → Minor → Solid → **Major +5**), via the universal **Hardened Save** feat (see the Feats document). So **Major is reachable only by feat investment, and only on a Save your gateway already set to Solid (+3)** — your best Saves are the ones you can push to exceptional, and a feat can also patch a hindrance up toward neutral (−3 → 0, never spiking a mandatory soft spot to exceptional). The untrained baseline is the gateway profile; feats are the trained, **flat, always-on** spike on top — a permanent step up the Modifier Ladder, added to the Save exactly as the gateway profile step is.

**Save height is the one sanctioned feat-bought height in KD20** (see *Where Bonuses Come From* — feats otherwise never sell flat bonuses). It is allowed only because it is *defensive* (it never drives the margin/DC treadmill) and *gated by the profile* (the Solid-save requirement for Major, and the lift-toward-neutral-only cap on a hindrance, do the rationing). It is therefore costed **above a cluster feat**, on a **scaled ladder** that makes patching a weakness cheap and reaching exceptional dear:

| Step | From → To | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Patch a hindrance | **−3 → 0** | **20** XP |
| Train a neutral Save | **0 → +1** (Minor) | **20** XP |
| Spike toward strong | **+1 → +3** (Solid) | **30** XP |
| Reach exceptional | **+3 → +5** (Major) | **40** XP *(gateway-Solid Save only)* |

A step is bought from the Save's *current* value, so climbing 0 → +3 is two purchases. *(Distinct from a **Specialization Die on a Save**, which a few gateway feats grant — e.g. Steel Discipline, Iron Aegis — as a conditional rider on a feat that does more; that die is not this flat height, and both can apply.)*

**The five built gateways' Save profiles** (by stat; read each column as that stat's Save):

| Gateway | Str Save | Dex Save | Con Save | Int Save | Wis Save | Cha Save |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Warrior** | +3 | +1 | +3 | 0 | 0 | **−3** |
| **Scout** | +1 | +3 | 0 | 0 | +3 | **−3** |
| **Adept** | 0 | 0 | **−3** | +3 | +3 | +1 |
| **Guardian** | +3 | **−3** | +3 | 0 | 0 | +1 |
| **Face** | 0 | 0 | **−3** | +3 | +1 | +3 |

*The profile tells a story — now with the force-of-will reading of Charisma: the **Warrior** is strong and hardy but shakeable of will (Cha Save −3: brave in body, but fear and domination find the crack); the **Scout** is nimble and keen-eyed but awkward under social pressure (Cha Save −3); the **Adept** has a fortified, disciplined mind and clear perception but a frail body (Con Save −3); the **Guardian** is an immovable bulwark that cannot dodge (Dex Save −3); the **Face** is iron-willed and sharp-minded but no fighter's constitution (Con Save −3).*

*(Note on the Warrior: its old soft spot was "Resolve −3," which under the new coverage is the **Charisma Save** — so fear/domination/"force of will" is still the Warrior's crack, exactly as before. The reassignment preserved every gateway's intended weakness; only the label on the column changed, except the Warrior's Minor/none pairing, which now reads Dex Save +1 / Wis Save 0 — strong footwork, ordinary read of a room — matching the martial fantasy.)*

**Effects scale with the attacker's success tier** (Success / Decisive / Supreme) the same way damage does — see *Effect Escalation* below.

### Capability Roll-Types — Named Stat-Rolls, Not a Skill List

KD20 retired the skill list; it did **not** replace it with a new list. There is no "Persuasion skill," no "Perception skill" — there is **Charisma, used to persuade** and **Wisdom, used to perceive.** A *capability roll-type* is simply a **named stat-roll**: a convenient name for "roll this stat, applied to this kind of thing." The name does two jobs and only two — it tells you **which stat** governs, and it **sets the fiction** (and so shapes the consequences). It carries no number of its own; the bonus is the stat, period.

> **A capability roll is `1d20 + stat + (a Specialization Die, if a feat's trigger fires)`** — the same anatomy as every KD20 roll. "A Persuasion roll" *is* a Charisma roll; "a Perception roll" *is* a Wisdom roll. The three-tier stack applies: anyone may attempt it (base `d20 + stat`), a **feat** trains it (the d6 on its trigger), and Action Dice push it (the gradient).

**The GM names the stat at the table — from the stat definitions, not a lookup.** Because *The Character of each Statistic* (above) says what each stat governs, the GM answers "what do I roll to do X?" by asking **"which stat does this lean on?"** and reading it off the fiction. This is the whole mechanism. KD20 deliberately does **not** try to name every possible action — a master list is exactly the skill list the Capabilities cut removed. The guide below is a **baseline for the common cases and the activities the feat library references by name** — a starting point and a consistency anchor, **not an exhaustive registry.** When something isn't on it, the GM assigns a stat from the definitions and plays on; nothing is "missing."

**The baseline guide** (common roll-types the feats reference — default stat, and what it covers; all overridable per *Rerouting*, below):

| Roll-type | Default stat | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Perception** | wis | noticing, spotting, searching, reading a scene |
| **Insight** | wis | reading intent, sensing a lie, gauging a person |
| **Stealth** | dex | moving unseen or unheard, hiding |
| **Tracking / Survival** | wis | following trails, foraging, weather, living off the land |
| **Persuasion** | cha | convincing, negotiating, charming |
| **Presence** | cha | commanding, intimidating, performing, holding a room |
| **Deception** | cha | lying, disguise, running a con |
| **Athletics** | str | climbing, jumping, swimming, forcing, hauling |
| **Acrobatics** | dex | balance, tumbling, squeezing through, sleight of hand |
| **Lore / Academics** | int | recalling knowledge, languages, deduction, arcane theory |
| **Craft** | int | building, repairing, engineering, fine work |
| **Medicine** | int or wis | treating wounds, diagnosing, aiding Recovery *(GM's call which, by approach)* |
| **Spellcasting** | **gateway-determined** | casting a spell-feat — **the gateway sets the stat** (Adept = int; a faith caller = wis; an innate/sorcerous caller = cha) |

*Spellcasting is the clearest proof that a roll-type is just a stat-plus-fiction: the same act (casting) hangs off different stats depending on* how *your calling works magic — studied (int), devout/intuited (wis), or innate force of self (cha). The gateway declares it; the roll-type name just means "your casting stat."*

**Rerouting a roll-type — the offensive mirror of the Fitting Save.** The listed stat is the **default**, not a cage. Exactly as a defender may route a threat through a different Save when the fiction earns it (see *The Fitting Save*), **an actor who credibly describes a different approach to a task may, with GM permission, use a different stat for it.** Intimidate a guard with a looming display of **Strength** instead of **Charisma**; pick a lock by patient **Wisdom** (feel and intuition) rather than deft **Dexterity**; recall a route by **Intelligence** (you studied the map) or by **Wisdom** (you've walked it). The same guard-rails apply as the Fitting Save: **the fiction leads and the GM decides** (permission granted, not picked; the GM's "no" is fast and final); you use **one stat, not best-of**; the approach is **earned by commitment**, and **its consequences follow the method you chose** — intimidating by force may cow this guard but make an enemy, where charm would have made a contact. One principle, both halves of the game: *the fiction can always move which stat you roll, offense or defense, at the GM's word.*

**"A [roll-type] feat"** — wherever the feat library gives a prerequisite like "requires a Perception feat" or "an Insight feat," it means **any feat that grants a Specialization Die on that roll-type.** The roll-type names the *competence*; the feat is the *trained investment* in it. (So "requires a Perception feat" is satisfied by Eagle Eye, Battle Awareness, or any future feat that trains perceiving.) Base competence needs no feat — anyone rolls `d20 + stat`; the feat is the trained spike, exactly as everywhere else.

## Actors and Construction Tracks

### The Character Track

Actors built with the full **character-creation rules** are on the Character track. This includes every PC, and any NPC the GM chooses to build to that standard — a major recurring villain, a party-allied NPC of real importance.

Character-track actors:

- **Own an Action Dice pool** of their own, sized and refreshed by the normal rules. A PC-built villain has their own pool; the GM operates it on the villain's behalf. A PC-built ally has their own pool too.
- **Obey the action economy** (1 Move + 1 Action per round — see *The Action Economy*).
- Are built the same way regardless of who runs them. A character is a character.

### The Threat Track

Most adversaries — monsters, minions, mooks, incidental NPCs — are **not** built as characters. They are authored on the Threat track: a separate, GM-friendly construction method (the **Threat Design System**, a later section) that produces compact stat blocks tuned for the table, not full character sheets.

Threat-track actors:

- **Do not carry an Action Dice pool, and the GM has none either.** A threat's difficulty is built into its design, not propped up by spent dice — a threat is made as tough or as fragile as the scene needs through its own stat block and abilities.
- **Are built differently from characters**, with bespoke abilities that fire on their own logic rather than by spending Action Dice.
- **Are not bound by the character action economy.** A threat-track creature may have a stat block that grants extra actions, reactions, or movement freely — its capabilities are defined by its design, not by the 1-Action-1-Move rule.

This split is what keeps GM bookkeeping sane: the GM tracks, at most, the pools of a handful of named character-track actors — not a pool per creature in every fight, and no pool of their own to manage.

### The Universal Actor Baseline

Regardless of track, every actor shares a common chassis of stats — the things that let any two actors interact in the same combat. **This baseline is universal:**

- **Armour Class (AC)** — how hard the actor is to hit. **AC = 10 + dex + armour + shield + misc.** DEX always applies, regardless of armour type (well-fitted armour distributes its load; it does not cap agility). Armour and shields add on top. Typical characters sit in a **static band of ~13–17** (naked ~10, agile-and-armoured veteran ~20), and **that band holds for the whole campaign** — see the item note below. *(AC is the one **static** defense — the attacker rolls against it. The six **Saves** — Strength / Dexterity / Constitution / Intelligence / Wisdom / Charisma Save — are **rolled by the defender**; see Defenses, above.)*
  - **The armour table** (the small closed set):

    | Category | AC bonus | Examples |
    |---|---|---|
    | None (unarmored) | +0 | — |
    | **Light** | **+1** | padded, leather, hide |
    | **Medium** | **+2** | chain, scale, brigandine |
    | **Heavy** | **+3** | plate, banded |
    | **Shield** (any) | **+1** | carried alongside any armour |

    The values are deliberately **tight** because the **Armour Training** feat adds a further **+1 / +2** on top (it requires medium or heavy armour), and **AC-feat height has to stay in band.** Ceiling check: a maxed heavy tank (dex +4, plate +3, shield +1, Armour Training +2) reaches **AC 20** — the top of the band; only a dex-20 extreme touches 21, which the **~20 soft cap** (GM-upheld, not clamped) absorbs. **Dex always applies at every category** — KD20 never caps agility by armour, which is *why* the base values are small (a high-dex heavy build is the one the cap watches). The **category** also gates the AC feats: Armour Training needs medium/heavy worn; **Unarmored Defense** needs light or none (see the Feats document). *(Armour is carried as **equippable items**; the category and bonus live on the item.)*
- **Hit Points (HP)** — how much punishment the actor can take before going down. HP is **abstracted**, not a measure of flesh: it folds together stamina, combat experience, the instinct to not be where the blow lands, luck, divine interest, and every other "soft" factor that keeps a capable actor in the fight. A low HP total means *little of that buffer banked yet* — which is why a starting character is fragile and a veteran is not, without either being made of different meat. (This is Pillar 1 in the health system: the buffer is earned.)
  - **HP = 10 + con + wis + gateway bonus.** The gateway bonus reflects a calling's durability (provisionally ~+2 for fragile callings like scholars/mages, ~+4 for mid ones like rogues/scouts, ~+6 for hardy ones like warriors/guardians). Starting characters land roughly **13–20**. DEX is deliberately *not* in HP — DEX avoids the hit (AC), while con+wis absorb or slip it (HP).
  - **HP feats** (deferred to the feat system) let a character fold an additional stat's bonus into HP — one feat per stat, each bought once, each granting that stat's *living* bonus (it tracks the stat), and each requiring the stat at **16+**. Bulwark (+con), I See It Coming (+wis), NOT IN THE FACE! (+cha, force of personality), and so on. Buy-once-each caps the total; the 16+ gate ties durability growth to stat growth (which is GM-catalyst-gated), so HP earns in over the campaign.
- **Damage** — the dice an actor's attacks roll (see The Damage Model, below)

**Magic items grant capabilities, not statistics.** KD20 avoids +1/+2/+3 gear. If a magic item carries a numerical bonus at all it is small (at most +1) and always secondary to a distinctive *feature* — a sword that trembles when someone means you harm, a shield with a lion's head that bites an attacker who misses. This is what keeps AC a static, campaign-long band and prevents the treadmill: with no bonus-creep from items, the goalpost never moves, so growing characters get *better at reaching it* (the margin ladder blooms) rather than facing ever-higher targets.

*(AC and HP numbers above are **provisional**, to be validated in playtest — the HP-to-damage ratio especially, see the Playtest Watch-List. The armour/shield table is now defined — see above — and validated against the hit-chance band (a trained master holds AC 20 to ~47% passive; the pool lifts it over 50%). Weapon values remain a later table.)*

### The Damage Model

An attack that hits rolls its **damage die** and adds the appropriate **stat bonus**. On a Decisive or Supreme hit, it *also* rolls **Precision Dice** (below).

**Damage dice** run from **d6 to d12** for weapons. **Unarmed strikes** deal a **d4** for most humanoids.

**Which stat bonus modifies damage** depends on the weapon or attack:

- **str** — most melee weapons and unarmed strikes.
- **dex** — finesse melee weapons (e.g. a rapier) and most ranged weapons.
- **Other** — magical and special attacks use whatever bonus their own description names.

*(The full weapon table — each weapon's damage die, Precision Die, stat bonus, and properties — is a later section. The model here is what makes an actor's attacks expressible for testing: a damage die, a stat bonus, and a Precision Die.)*

### The Precision Die

Most hits deal only damage dice. But a **Decisive** or **Supreme** hit can add **one Precision Die** to the damage roll — the reward for landing a blow that found something vital. It is always a *single* die: at Decisive you choose it *or* a Follow-Up; at Supreme you get it *and* a Follow-Up (see *Decisive and Supreme Rewards*). There is no doubled-damage tier.

A Precision Die is a **separate, tunable property of each weapon**, distinct from its damage die:

- **By default, a weapon's Precision Die equals its damage die.** A longsword dealing d8 damage adds a d8 Precision Die on a Decisive hit.
- **Some weapons deliberately differ**, and that difference *is* their identity. A dagger deals only a **d6** on a casual hit — but it is built for the placed strike, so its Precision Die is a **d12**. A glancing dagger is minor; a Decisive one is fearsome.

This gives every weapon two independent design levers: the **damage die** (what it does when it merely connects) and the **Precision Die** (what it does when you truly land it). A heavy weapon might be a consistent thumper (big damage die, ordinary Precision Die); an assassin's tool might be unremarkable until it isn't (small damage die, oversized Precision Die).

### First Weapon (seed entry)

The full weapon table is deferred with the equipment layer, but the first entry is set as a testing anchor and to establish the stat-block shape:

**Short Sword** — Damage **d6** · Precision Die **d10** · Reach **1 unit (2 m)** · Stat **str or dex** (finesse) · Properties: **Parry**

- **Parry** *(property, provisional):* while wielding this weapon and able to react, you may spend **1 Action Die** to react to an incoming melee attack and **defend with a contest** — roll 1d20 + your relevant bonus against the attacker's attack total. **Win, and the attack is turned aside** (no damage). **Win Decisively** (beat their attack total by 10+) and you may take a **Follow-Up** — the classic riposte, a free counter-attack, where the fiction and your weapon allow (see *Decisive and Supreme Rewards*). Like any reaction it costs the die and must flow from the trigger (an attack you could plausibly parry — not a boulder, not an arrow from behind). *(Exact wording lives in the weapon/equipment layer; this stub gives Parry meaning so the short sword is testable. Parry is a contest because a parry* is *opposed — blade against blade — so it reuses KD20's contest machinery and scales with the defender's skill automatically.)*

*(The short sword takes str **or** dex — a finesse weapon — matching the damage model's finesse category. Its d10 Precision Die exceeds its d6 damage: an unremarkable blade that bites hard on a placed strike, a lighter cousin of the dagger's d6/d12.)*

## Hit Points, Recovery, and Defeat

KD20's health model rests on one idea, set at the Universal Actor Baseline: **HP is not meat.** It is the buffer of experience, luck, grit, ducking and weaving that keeps a capable actor in the fight. Losing HP costs nothing physical — so it comes back fast. The body only truly pays when the buffer runs out: **reaching 0 HP is where injury and lasting consequence happen, not before.**

### Recovery — HP Comes Back Fast

Because HP is abstract, it recovers on a short breather. **Fifteen minutes of rest with no fighting restores a character to full HP.** No roll, no resource, no tracking of partial healing between fights — the composure returns, the luck resets, the second wind arrives.

This keeps the action moving: once a fight is over and the party has a moment, HP is a non-issue. But fifteen minutes is long enough that it *cannot* be claimed under pressure — a second fight hard on the heels of the first still finds you depleted, so HP matters *during* a push even though it stops mattering once the push is over.

**Fast HP recovery does not undo a defeat consequence.** Restoring HP refills the buffer; it does not heal the *meat injury* a character takes on reaching 0 (below). Those lasting wounds heal by the slower Recovery system *(deferred)* — a Constitution-based process others may aid with Medicine (see *The Modifier Ladder — Aiding Recovery*). HP is cheap and fast; injuries are not. The split is the whole point: nothing lasting ever comes from *losing* HP, only from *running out* of it.

**Adrenaline Rush** (a martial feat — see the Feats document) is the one way to recover HP *during* combat: a fighter may spend an Action Die, once per combat, to heal the die's roll — topping the buffer back up mid-fight rather than waiting for the breather.

### Defeat — Reaching 0 HP

**A character reduced to 0 HP is defeated, not automatically dead.** At that moment the **player chooses a Defeat Option, subject to GM approval.** The player authors how their hero goes down; the GM gates it against the fiction (you cannot, for instance, choose an option the situation doesn't allow). This is the big sibling of the Hard Choice: at the worst moment, the player keeps agency over their own downfall. Defeat is also **where the "meat" finally happens** — the lasting consequence that fast HP recovery deliberately never carries.

*(Before 0: some abilities let a character avoid defeat rather than face it — e.g. the Warrior's **Iron Resilience**, spending an Action Die to stay standing at 1 HP. Those fire* before *this choice; Defeat Options begin once 0 is actually reached.)*

**The default Defeat Options:**

- **Take the Wound** — you stay conscious and in the fight, but the blow *lands for real*: you suffer a **lasting injury**, a Permission- or Modifier-lever **State** that does not heal on a fifteen-minute rest (it requires the slower Recovery system). You are at 1 HP and may act. *This is the option that makes "0 is where the body pays" concrete — survival bought with meat.*
- **Go Down** *(default)* — you fall unconscious but alive, helpless and at the mercy of the scene. The **Golden Hour** begins (below). Choosing this signals you want to keep playing this character. It might be time to plan a prison escape.
- **Last Stand** — you refuse to fall yet. Make a **stabilisation roll** (see below); on a Success you fight on for 2–3 rounds with a **Specialization Die on your attacks** (borrowed time, and the dice notice the bold), after which your character dies. No Golden Hour — this was a last stand, not a recoverable wound. On a Failure you simply **Go Down** instead.
- **Clean Exit** — you accept death immediately and cleanly: no lingering, no complication. The sacrifice means something — your next character begins with a meaningful XP bonus reflecting the moment.
- **Vendetta** — you die, but refuse to stay dead, returning as a revenant bent on the one who killed you. Your character becomes a recurring force in the narrative, an NPC in the GM's hands, laid to rest only by an extended **ritual** (see the ritual-as-treasure framework). The most dramatic long-term option.

**The stabilisation roll** (used by some options) is an ordinary KD20 roll — `1d20 + con` (or wis, as the fiction suits) against a Moderate DC, read on the outcome ladder: a Success stabilises; a **Decisive** does better (stabilise *and* stay conscious, helpless but able to speak); a Failure slips to the worse branch the option names.

**The Golden Hour.** A character who has Gone Down (or otherwise lies gravely defeated) is on a clock: left untreated, their condition worsens toward death across the scene; treated in time — by an ally's Medicine, a heal, the party getting them to safety — they pull through, though a lasting injury (a Wound State) may remain. *The lasting harm of defeat is largely in the party's hands: a defeated character survives whole if their friends act, and bears the scar if they don't.* (Exact Golden-Hour timing and the Wound/injury catalogue belong with the deferred Recovery and conditions systems; the principle is fixed here.)

### Campaign-Specific Defeat Options

The five options above are the **default menu**. A campaign may cut, alter, or add to them to fit its tone — a grim game might remove Clean Exit's clean comfort; a high-fantasy one might add resurrection; a specific setting might add an option born of its own cosmology. **Such changes live in that campaign's own document, not in these core rules.** Core defines the *mechanism* (0 = defeated; player chooses with GM approval; defeat is where lasting consequence enters) and the default menu; the campaign layer customizes the menu.

## The Action Economy

This governs **character-track** actors. Threat-track actors are not bound by it — their stat blocks define what they can do (see Actors and Construction Tracks).

### Initiative

Turn order is **rolled fresh each round**, so the flow of a fight shifts round to round rather than locking on turn one. Each actor rolls **1d20 + the better of their dex or wis bonus** — quick reflexes *or* sharp awareness can put you first. Highest acts first, descending. Ties are broken by the higher of the two bonuses; if still tied, the actors act simultaneously (or the GM calls it). Threat-track actors roll the same way unless their stat block says otherwise.

### Move and Action

Each combat round, a character gets **one Move and one Action.**

- **Move** — locomotion by whatever means the character has (walk, fly, swim, climb, burrow). A human-sized character moves **6 grid units** per round.
- **Action** — the thing you *do*: an attack, a maneuver, casting, a skill use, and so on.
- **You may trade your Action *down* for a second Move** (2 Moves, no Action → 12 units of movement). **You may not trade a Move up into an Action.** Movement is cheap; action is precious.
- **A single Move may be split around your Action** — move part of it, act, then move the rest (e.g. move 3 units, attack, move 3 units away).

### Distance and the Grid

KD20 measures distance in **concrete units** — a **2-metre grid**, 6 units (12 m) per Move. This is the default because concrete distance is universally legible: grid tables and theatre-of-the-mind tables alike understand "6 metres away," whereas abstract range-bands are a learned dialect. Tables that prefer looser play simply narrate the distances; the grid is the assumed default, not a requirement. (**Metres and yards are interchangeable** at this scale — read every "metre" as "yard" if you prefer imperial; the ~9% difference never matters in play.)

### Reactions

A character may **react** — act outside their own turn — by spending **1 Action Die.** A reaction:

- **Costs only the Action Die** — it does not touch your own Move/Action for the round. It is a separate, die-purchased off-turn beat.
- **Must flow from the triggering event**, exactly as a Follow-Up must flow from its spawning action. If a foe flees your reach you may react with a swing or a grab — something that *fits* the trigger. You cannot react with an unrelated act.
- **Is uncapped, but one trigger yields one reaction.** With dice and distinct triggers, you may react repeatedly across a round — but a single event (one fleeing foe) allows one reaction, and you cannot stack multiple reactions onto it. Two fleeing foes are two triggers; they do not license three reactions against one of them.

### Zone of Control

An actor's **Zone of Control** is the reach of their weapon — by default **1 grid unit in all directions** (extending for reach weapons). **Leaving** an enemy's Zone of Control — stepping out of their reach — lets that enemy react. Moving *within* it does not provoke; only escaping it does. You can always leave, but you may pay a toll for it.

The provoke is just a normal reaction: a **character-track** enemy spends **1 Action Die** to take the swing; a **threat-track** enemy uses whatever the reaction its stat block grants. Nothing new — the "attack of opportunity" is simply a reaction triggered by leaving reach.

### Universal Maneuvers

Some tactical actions are **universal** — any actor may attempt them, on any track, without a feat or gateway: **shove, trip, grapple, disarm, throw sand in the eyes,** and their kin. A maneuver is taken as your Action and resolved as a **contest** (your roll vs. the target's resisting roll), read on the outcome ladder and modifiable by Action Dice like any roll.

- Being *exceptional* at a maneuver — the Monk's grappling, a Brawler's throws — comes from a gateway/feat **Specialization Die** layered on the universal attempt. The maneuver itself is never gated; only mastery is.
- A maneuver is also what a combat **Follow-Up** can trigger for free on a Decisive or Supreme hit (see *Decisive and Supreme Rewards*) — there it rides on the attack instead of costing your Action.

## Deferred Systems

Named and scoped here so they aren't reinvented or forgotten. Not yet designed.

**Threat Design System** — the GM-friendly shorthand for authoring threat-track actors (monsters, minions, incidental NPCs) over the Universal Actor Baseline. Compact stat blocks, bespoke abilities, no Action Dice pool, free of the character action economy. Deferred until the major character-and-combat subsystems have settled, so the shorthand compresses finished rules rather than moving ones.

**Specialization system** — the single capability pattern in KD20: a feat with a **narrowly-defined trigger** that grants a **Specialization Die** (a fixed **d6**) on the roll or damage when its circumstance is met. Not a pool resource — not spent, banked, or refreshed; it simply appears when you make the move you trained for. **Only one Specialization Die applies to any roll** (owning more feats is broader coverage, not a bigger bonus); when several could apply, the chosen feat sets the fiction. Gear advertises its specialization hooks in its own stats, and a matching feat unlocks them (e.g. a Great Sword lists *Sweep*; the feat **Sweep: Great Sword** grants a d6 whenever the wielder sweeps to strike all targets in the weapon's reach with one roll). Distinct from Action Dice (pool) and Precision Dice (combat-hit reward). Feats are **buy-once** and advance the character through *breadth* (more triggers), never die-size. The one depth mechanism is the **Master Feat** — once, permanently, narrow one held feat to a sharper trigger for a *second* d6 there (the only place two Specialization Dice stack); one per character, no retraining, no refund. *(Full catalog lives in the feat library, deferred; the architecture is fixed: narrow trigger → conditional d6, gear-hook + feat-unlock, one-die-applies, breadth-not-size, single permanent Master Feat.)*

**Feat system** — the broader framework the Specialization pattern and the dice-granting feats (e.g. "Luck") live in. Feats are bought with XP (see *XP as universal currency*). Deferred; the earning and specialization hooks referenced above are fixed, the catalog is not.

**Gateway system (the one capability structure)** — KD20's replacement for character classes *and* skills alike. A **Gateway is an identity** — a calling, a profession, or a culture (Warrior, Scout, Wizard, Mercenary, Courtesan, Leader, Warrior of the Polar Bear Clan). Combat callings, civilian trades, and cultures are all the *same object*: a gateway with a themed cluster of narrow d6 feats. There is no separate class system, skill system, and profession system — there is one system worn in many costumes. Structure:

- A **Gateway feat** is the door to an identity. Taking it declares a character's niche, grants a modest base benefit, and **unlocks that gateway's cluster feats**, which are otherwise inaccessible.
- **Cluster feats** are the narrow d6-on-trigger capabilities inside it — the "skills" of the old model, now denominated in the atom. They form a shallow, wide tree: taking the gateway opens a **first ring** (pick one or more as a starting ability, XP permitting); each first-ring feat opens a **second ring** of related feats. *Example: Warrior gateway → Weapon Master (Rapier) [+d6 to attack rolls with a rapier; requires Dexterity 15] → from it, Riposte / Parry / Intimidate, etc.*
- **Feats live in a shared library; a gateway is a curated menu into it plus a few signature feats.** Overlapping gateways (Mercenary and City Watch both offering *Battlefield Nerve*) draw the *same* authored feat rather than duplicating it — so "many specific gateways" is cheap to author and free of copy-paste maintenance. What distinguishes two gateways is their combination of library feats plus their handful of originals.
- The tree is kept **shallow and wide by design guideline, not hard wall**. Growth is **breadth, not tiers**: no "Weapon Master I / II / III" die-inflation — you expand into new related feats, never upgrade one to a bigger die. The sole depth mechanism is the **Master Feat**.
- **The Master Feat** — once per character, permanently, narrow one feat you hold to a sharper signature trigger and grant *that* a second d6 (the only place two Specialization Dice stack). No retraining, no refund: mastery is a lifelong pursuit, and its permanence protects player niches (a claimed specialty stays claimed; the party diversifies rather than converging). Choose wisely.
- **Combat cluster feats add their Specialization Die to the *attack roll*** (hit better → climb the ladder → more Decisive/Supreme → more Precision Dice), never a flat +damage. Raw damage spikes come from exploding damage dice, available to all.
- **Gateways are soft-mandatory**: not strictly required, but the cluster-feat access and niche-signalling make one all but essential, and a GM may simply require one.
- **Multiple gateways — allowed, but rare, earned, and breadth-only (never a power-stack).** A character may take a second (or third) gateway, but it is deliberately costly and grants *access*, not stacked numbers — so single-gateway stays the strong default and multi-gateway is a character-driven breadth choice, not an optimization. The rules:
  - **Acquisition: narrative catalyst + XP.** Like raising a Statistic, a new gateway must be *earned in the fiction* (a GM-gated catalyst — a mentor, a conversion, a crucible) and then paid for with the full **gateway cost (30 XP)**. You cannot simply buy a second calling; the story must open the door.
  - **Primary gateway = your first, fixed forever.** It sets your **defense profile**, which **never stacks** — a second gateway does *not* grant a second profile, so you cannot erase your primary gateway's mandatory hindrance by stacking. Your soft spot stays. (You *can* patch it over time by buying the new gateway's defense-improvement feats — earned, paid, gradual, never free.)
  - **HP bonus: highest only.** You use the best HP bonus among your gateways, never the sum.
  - **What you gain:** each additional gateway's **benefit** (its always-on ability) and **access to its cluster feats**. This is the real draw — more tools, more options, a whole new capability axis (a Warrior/Adept can now cast) — pure *breadth*, not bigger numbers. Where a gateway benefit includes an Action-Die boost-floor, each applies only to its *own* signature act (melee vs. spell, etc.) and they never combine on a single roll.
  - **Capstone feats ("requires any three other [Gateway] feats") count only feats of that specific gateway.** Depth in a calling isn't shortcut by breadth across callings — a dabbler-in-two reaches neither calling's capstones as easily. This is the self-balancing cost of spreading wide.
  - **Focus has its own reward — the counterweight to breadth.** A character who stays **locked to a single gateway** can take the universal **Singular Path** feat (requires the **Deep Well** pool feat first), gaining two extra Action Dice usable only on actions within their gateway's theme — mastery of one calling as the mirror of the access a second gateway grants (see the Feats document). The lock is breakable: taking a second gateway later forfeits Singular Path and its dice, with **no XP refund** (above). So advancement forks cleanly: **breadth** (a second gateway — more tools, no stacked power) or **focus** (devotion — deeper mastery of one), each earned, each with a real trade-off.
- **Attack capability is feat-driven, like all capability.** Anyone may attack at `1d20 + stat` (flat, no penalty for using any weapon) — the same base-competence floor every task uses; combat *superiority* comes from cluster feats granting Specialization Dice on triggers (Weapon Master (Rapier), a Monk's strikes). This keeps AC static (protecting the margin ladder) and makes "good at combat" a niche identity you invest in, exactly like being good at anything else. Each gateway defines its own **weapon/technique menu** (the Rogue's short list, the Cleric's cult weapon, the Warrior's broad access) — nuance lives in the cluster. Unarmed *striking* works the same way. **Maneuvers (grapple, shove, trip, disarm) are universal** (see *Universal Maneuvers*) — anyone may attempt them; what a gateway grants is a **Specialization Die** on those maneuvers (the Monk's grappling, a Brawler's throws), layered on the universal baseline.
- **Cultures are gateways too (Session Zero opt-in).** Because a gateway *is* an identity, a culture ("Warrior of the Polar Bear Clan") is simply a gateway — or a themed sub-menu within one — whose library selection includes some shared feats and some culture-signature originals (Bear's Endurance, a clan-weapon specialization). Tables that want cultural identity turn it on at Session Zero; the GM authors it the same way as any gateway (curate a menu, add a few signatures). Off by default; generic-fantasy tables never see a cultural creation step. This was the prototype that proved the whole gateway-as-identity pattern.
- *(Verify each gateway is a KD20 original as it's built. The **Leader** gateway — a calling built around Action Dice command: doling out dice from its own pool to allies, earning dice for outstanding leadership — is the first named example; its closest resource analogues are Face and Guardian, not a dedicated commander calling.)*

**XP as universal currency** — nearly everything except ordinary gear is bought with XP: gateway feats, cluster feats, the Master Feat, resist feats, and even starting advantages framed as feats (an *Heirloom Weapon*, an *Income* feat scaled by starting monthly wealth). Character creation and advancement are the same act — spending XP — at different times, not two separate systems. **The cluster feat is the pricing peg** — the atomic unit of the economy, mirroring the d6 as the atomic unit of mechanics. Provisional, simulation-supported costs: **cluster feat 20 · gateway feat 30 · Master Feat 40**. The one exception is **Statistic increases**, which are *not* an open XP purchase — they are GM-gated by a narrative catalyst and then consolidated for a low XP cost (~10; see *Raising a Statistic*). *(The full feat library and advancement framework are deferred content; the costing peg and structure are fixed here.)*

**The feat library and gateway roster (content layer)** — the actual catalog of cluster feats and the starting gateways (professions/callings/cultures). Cluster feats are narrow d6-on-trigger capabilities, authored once in a shared library; **a gateway is a curated menu into that library plus a few signature feats** unique to it, so overlapping gateways (a Mercenary and a City Watch both offering *Battlefield Nerve*) share one authored feat rather than duplicating it. This makes many specific gateways cheap to author and free of copy-paste maintenance. Some feats exist only in settings that have them (*Firearms* only where gunpowder exists; formal vs. folk medicine). The roll formula, the one-die rule, the Master Feat, and the costing peg are fixed in the core; the library and roster are per-setting content. **Note:** attacking and universal maneuvers are *not* feats you need in order to attempt them (anyone acts at `d20 + stat`); a combat feat grants the *Specialization Die* on its trigger. *(This is the big content build; the MYD20 feat catalogue is the mining stock — see the Feat Translation Guide.)*

**Size Scale** — a scale centered at **0 = human**, with steps up (ogre, giant, and larger) and down (halfling, sprite, and smaller). Its numeric effects are **additive and upward-only**: a size *above* human adds a flat modifier to **damage rolls** and to **HP** (flat additions, never die-size changes). Human size and everything *below* it take **no** size modifier to damage or HP — being smaller than human makes an actor *different* (nimble, slippery, expressed through its own abilities), not *worse*. **AC is untouched by size** at every step, since larger-than-human actors are authored with threat rules and simply have their AC set directly. Primarily a threat/NPC tool, consistent with "exceptional nature is earned, not chosen." *(Exact steps and per-step values live inside the Threat Design System.)*

## Playtest Watch-List

Predictions logged *before* play, checked against ready-made levers rather than discovering problems cold. **A first-pass Monte Carlo simulation (v0.23, real RNG) has now been run** — a starting character ("Bram": STR/DEX 14, CON/WIS 12–13, Warrior gateway, short sword, AC 14, HP 18) against a matched threat, plus 100k-roll outcome-distribution sweeps. Findings are folded in below and marked **[SIM]**. These remain provisional pending *live-table* play, which tests *feel* (tone, fun) as the sim cannot.

**0. Defenses — rolled Saves CONFIRMED well-received at the table.** Live play of the static-AC / rolled-Saves model landed well — players wanted the dice in their hands and the model delivers. The one piece of feedback was on the *names* of the saves and what they imply; resolved in v0.36 by renaming the six Saves to bare stat names (Strength Save, Dexterity Save, …) and re-dividing the three mental Saves (Int = mind, Wis = perception, Cha = will). The **Fitting Save** rule (a described defense may route a threat through a different stat's Save, GM-granted, consequences intact) was added in the same pass. *Watch:* whether the Fitting Save stays a crisp GM tool or starts generating table-debate (the "GM's no is fast and final" guard is the lever if it drifts).

**1. HP / lethality — prediction CONFIRMED, but not broken. [SIM]** Combat is fast and lethal: a matched no-Action-Dice duel averages ~5.5 rounds (range 2–14), and 2–4 solid hits drop a starting character. *But* the better-built side still wins reliably (~61/39 over 2000 fights), so it is **fast-and-deadly-and-high-variance, not a coin-flip.** No emergency. *Decision deferred to live play, which alone can judge tone:* if fast-and-lethal is the desired feel, leave it; if it feels too swingy at the table, the lever is a modest **HP bump (~20–25%)** (raise base or gateway bonus). The Precision Die is already capped at one die per hit, so it is *not* the culprit — HP scale is the only dial in question. Diagnose from *feel*, not the sim.

**2. Zero-to-hero curve — VALIDATED; my "too frequent" worry was wrong. [SIM]** Over 100k rolls the outcome ladder is near-perfect: Decisive fires **10% at starting (+4), 20% skilled (+6), 40% veteran (+10), 50% master (+13)**; Supreme stays **0% until master (5%)**. Decisive is a genuine achievement early and a signature of mastery late; Supreme is correctly legendary-rare. **This is the system's best-proven decision** — the margin ladder delivers Pillar 1 straight from the arithmetic, no tuning needed. Nothing to watch here except confirming it *feels* as good as it measures.

**3. Action Dice swing — RESOLVED: signature, not bug. Do NOT cap. [SIM]** The feared "dump the pool to buy a win" is **self-defeating**: steady 1-die-per-attack spending beats hoard-and-dump (86% vs 77% win rate over 2000 fights each), because spreading dice converts more hits into Decisives, while piling them on one attack *wastes overflow* past the +20 margin (nothing is buyable there). One Action Die is worth ~identical damage whether spent on the attack roll (4.67/rd) or hypothetically on damage (4.49/rd) — **no dead-zone, no reason for a damage-spend option.** The uncapped stack **self-regulates**: hoarding is a trap, tactical distribution is skill. **The spend-cap candidate is retired** — the math already does what a cap would, without removing the clutch-dump drama. The d6 stays (kept on table-feel grounds regardless). *Watch only:* whether, at a live table, the pool feels appropriately scarce given its sinks (boost / parry / reactions).

**4. DC-setting guidance — provisional band now exists and tested well. [SIM]** The margin ladder is sensitive (10 = Decisive), so consistent DCs matter. The band used in simulation produced the clean zero-to-hero curve above: **Easy 10 / Moderate 13 / Hard 16 / Formidable 20.** A starting character (+4) succeeds at Moderate ~60% with Decisive a real reach (~10%); a master (+13) trivializes Moderate and hits Decisive half the time. Strong provisional default — to be canonized with examples when the DC/guidance section is written. *Lever if live play disagrees:* shift the whole band up/down a point or two, but **preserve the ≈3-point spacing between tiers** — that spacing is what produced the good curve.

**5. Basic physical maneuvers — largely pre-solved.** Grappling/shoving/tripping are now **universal contests** any actor may attempt (see *Universal Maneuvers*), with specialization dice gated behind gateways. The earlier gap (a non-specialist having no hook for a simple shove) is closed. *Watch instead:* whether the universal maneuver contest *feels* good in play — clear to adjudicate, worth doing, not overshadowed by just attacking.

**6. Capabilities economy — VALIDATED at the proposed scale. [SIM]** The unified feat-die model (base d20+stat, one Specialization d6 on trigger, Action Dice as gradient) passed a Monte Carlo pass: trained beats untrained without a rank ladder (the d6 opens ~17% Decisive where untrained gets 0%); the zero-to-hero curve survives on a single non-stacking die; the untrained floor is playable at every DC tier (~65/50/35/15% at Easy/Mod/Hard/Formidable); and Action Dice give a smooth gradient (68→85→95→99% success spending 0→3 dice at Moderate). Costs greenlit at scale: **cluster feat 20 · gateway 30 · Master Feat 40 · stat-consolidation ~10.** *Watch:* Action Dice are potent (a trained specialist + 2 dice ≈ 95% success / 53% Decisive at Moderate) — not a flaw, but challenging content for trained characters should lean Hard/Formidable, and re-sim once the actual feat library exists.

**Meta-prediction — CONFIRMED. [SIM]** The prediction was that weak points would be in the *numbers*, not the *structure* — and the simulation bears this out completely. The **structure is sound**: the margin ladder, zero-to-hero curve, specialization dice (a Weapon Master beats an unspecialized mook 88/12), self-regulating Action Dice, and the unified Capabilities economy all behave as designed. The only genuinely open *number* question is **HP scale / combat lethality**, which is a *tone* decision the sim can't make — it must be judged at a live table. Everything else measured as intended or better.

*(First-pass findings are from Monte Carlo simulation, not live play. The sim validates *math*; only a real table validates *fun*. Re-run the sim after any numeric change to check the curve still holds.)*
